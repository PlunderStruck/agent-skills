# Extraction: design-of-everyday-things

**Source:** The Design of Everyday Things — Don Norman (Revised & Expanded Edition, Basic Books, 2013)

> Operational rules distilled from the source, written in our own words. Denser than the published skill; the place detail survives for re-distillation.

---

Norman's whole argument is that when a competent person fails at something simple — a door, a stove, a form, a confirmation dialog — the failure is legible in the design, not a fact about the person. For an agent building UI, the book supplies exact vocabulary for naming why an interaction will fail before it ships, plus a taxonomy of error that tells you which fix touches which failure — and, just as important, which fix does nothing at all against a failure that looks superficially similar.

## The seven stages of action and the two gulfs

Norman's diagnostic scaffold: any action cycle has seven stages — form a **goal**, **plan** an action, **specify** a sequence, **perform** it (the three execution stages), then **perceive** the resulting state of the world, **interpret** it, and **compare** it against the goal (the three evaluation stages). Between goal and execution sits the **Gulf of Execution**: the gap between what a person wants and the actions the system actually makes available and discoverable. Between the world's new state and understanding sits the **Gulf of Evaluation**: the effort needed to figure out what happened and whether it matches the goal. Every other concept in the book narrows one of these two gulfs: signifiers, mappings, and constraints narrow execution by making "what can I do and how" legible before acting; feedback and conceptual models narrow evaluation by making "what just happened" legible after acting. This is a real diagnostic move for a failing interaction: ask which of the seven stages is where the person actually gets stuck, and that tells you which category of fix is relevant — a feedback improvement does nothing for a Gulf-of-Execution failure, and vice versa. This is also where "feedforward" comes from as feedback's counterpart: feedback answers "what happened," feedforward answers "what can happen" before the person commits to an action.

## Affordance, signifier, perceived affordance — and why Norman regrets the original wording

This is the most misquoted pair of terms from the book, and Norman uses the revised edition specifically to correct it. An **affordance** is a relationship, not a property: it is what the properties of an object and the capabilities of an acting agent jointly make possible. A chair affords sitting for an adult; it does not afford lifting for a child too weak to lift it — for that child, the affordance does not exist, even though the chair's physical properties haven't changed. Affordances can be real but invisible (glass affords blocking air passage, which is why birds and distracted people fly into it) — an "anti-affordance" that hurts precisely because it isn't perceivable.

A **signifier** is Norman's fix for a confusion he watched spread through the design community after the first edition: designers began calling any visible mark that tells you where and how to act an "affordance" — "I put an affordance there" to mean a circle on a screen indicating where to tap. Norman argues this is a category error: the affordance of touching exists on the entire screen; the circle is a *signal* about where to do it, not a new capability for interaction. He says he tried to correct this usage, failed, and instead coined a new term for what designers actually meant: a signifier is any perceivable indicator — deliberate (a label, an arrow, a "push" plate) or accidental (a worn footpath, a pile of discarded cups signaling "you may put things here") — that communicates where and how to act. Signifiers can also actively mislead: a door handle that looks graspable but is purely decorative is a signifier that lies. His summary rule: affordances determine what actions are possible; signifiers communicate where the action should take place and that it is possible at all. Signifiers are almost always the actionable lever in practice: the underlying affordance (tap, click, drag) is usually already wired up; what's missing is the signal that tells the user it's there. When you build a swipeable card or a draggable handle, the absence of a signifier — nothing visually says "swipe here" — is what breaks discoverability, not the affordance. Concretely checkable: for any interactive element, ask not "can the user do this" but "is there a perceivable signal, independent of prior knowledge, that this specific action is possible here" — if the only source is a hover tooltip or tribal knowledge from a changelog, the signifier is missing even though the affordance exists.

## Mapping and natural mapping

A **mapping** is the relationship between a set of controls and the set of things they control. A mapping is **natural** when it exploits spatial or perceptual analogy so the relationship needs no learning: rotating a wheel clockwise to turn right, a seat-adjustment button shaped like the seat itself, a vertical slider where "up" means "more" (a near-universal, partly biological convention). Norman ranks natural mappings best to worst: controls mounted directly on the thing controlled; controls placed as close as possible to it; controls arranged in the same spatial configuration as the things they control (a floor-plan-shaped light switch panel). A mapping is **arbitrary** exactly when none of these hold and the only way to learn it is memorization or a label — his standing example is stove burners arranged in a rectangle controlled by a linear row of knobs: four different pairings are all in commercial use, none derivable from the layout, so every unfamiliar stove requires re-learning. This generalizes directly to software: any time controls and effects don't share a perceivable structural relationship — arbitrary tab order, a settings list whose groupings don't match how the options actually interact, a drag target far from what it affects — the mapping is arbitrary and costs a lookup every time, however "logical" it seemed to the person who built it. The default checkable question for any control layout: does the arrangement itself tell you which control does what, or do you need a label?

## Feedback: timing, and what makes it bad rather than absent

Feedback communicates the result of an action, and Norman insists it be evaluated on two axes most designers only check one of: **timing** and **quality** — failure on either is a distinct problem from simple absence of feedback. On timing: feedback delayed even a tenth of a second reads as unresponsive and invites the classic error of repeating the action, which can itself trigger a second, unintended effect if the first press actually registered. On quality, "bad feedback" is a real category distinct from "no feedback," failing in three specific ways: **too little** (a beep that says something happened but not what, so you can't tell which of several states resulted, or even which device made the sound); **too much** (his dishwasher beeping at 3am, or a "backseat driver" stream of correct-but-constant alerts that trains people to disable the whole channel, including the one alert that mattered); and **wrong channel/uninterpretable encoding** (a single light or tone forced to represent many states via a private flash-pattern code nobody can learn, made worse when every device on a panel uses an incompatible pattern). So "we show a toast" is not automatically "we gave feedback" — it has to be immediate, name what happened and to what object, be prioritized so routine confirmations don't drown the one that matters, and use a channel the user can attribute to the right source. Even ambient, incidental feedback matters: the buzzes and clicks that used to accompany a phone call connecting were themselves informative, and removing such cues during a redesign silently strips a confidence signal users didn't know they relied on.

## Constraints and forcing functions

Norman gives four kinds of constraint that narrow the space of plausible actions, distinguished by *where the restriction lives*. **Physical constraints** come from the object itself (a large peg cannot enter a small hole; a battery that only inserts one way) — but note his caution: a physical constraint only helps *before* an action if it's perceivable in advance, otherwise it merely blocks the wrong action *after* the attempt, which is worse than preventing the attempt (a symmetric battery compartment wired asymmetrically fails silently or damages the device once inserted wrong). **Cultural constraints** are learned, shared conventions — red conventionally means "brake," a headlight blink means "you go first" in England and "I'm going first" in Mexico, and the two collide when the same signal reaches someone from the other culture. **Semantic constraints** come from real-world meaning independent of culture (a windshield only makes sense in front of the rider it protects), and these can shift as technology changes what a signal needs to mean at all (a self-driving car's brake light may become meaningless once cars coordinate over a network instead of visually). **Logical constraints** are derivable by elimination — a leftover part after reassembly tells you, by logic alone, that it belongs somewhere; natural mapping is itself a special case of logical constraint, since spatial correspondence is a logical relationship, not a physical or cultural one.

**Forcing functions** are the strong end of physical constraint: situations engineered so that failing one step physically blocks the next. Norman names three variants. An **interlock** forces correct sequencing (a microwave that cuts power the instant the door opens; a car that won't leave Park without the brake depressed; a dead-man's switch that halts a machine if the operator's grip releases). A **lock-in** keeps a state active until a condition is deliberately satisfied — his "save before exit?" prompt example has become so reliable that he now uses it *as* his save mechanism, exiting and trusting the forced choice to catch unsaved work: a friction-adding forcing function became a shortcut once its behavior was internalized. A **lockout** prevents entry into a dangerous state altogether (a stairwell gate blocking a panicking crowd from running into a basement during a fire; the pin on a fire extinguisher). The concrete lesson for a destructive-action flow: a forcing function is the correct fix specifically for a **slip** — a failure where the goal was already right but execution or follow-through would otherwise go wrong (forgetting to remove a card before cash is dispensed). It is **not** a fix for a **mistake** — if the underlying plan is simply wrong, a forcing function that guarantees each step executes just makes the wrong plan execute more reliably. This connection — forcing functions patch execution, not diagnosis — is easy to misapply if you don't first identify which error category you're defending against.

Also checkable: **standardization** is explicitly Norman's "principle of desperation" — the fallback when no natural mapping is achievable (his single-lever mixer-faucet case: temperature and flow share one control with no natural spatial mapping, so the only fix is a cultural convention everyone learns once). This cuts both ways: treat it as a last resort when inventing a new interaction pattern, but as the *first* resort once a real platform convention already exists — inventing a novel gesture where an established one exists optimizes the wrong axis.

## The error taxonomy: slips vs. mistakes

This is the book's most load-bearing distinction, and Norman is precise that it splits along where in the seven-stage action cycle the failure occurs. **Human error** in general is any deviation from appropriate behavior, and it has exactly two top-level categories, each occurring at a different altitude of the action cycle.

A **slip** happens when the goal and plan are correct but execution or evaluation goes wrong — the action performed is not the one intended. Slips live in the bottom four stages (perform, perceive, interpret, and the transitions around them), governed by subconscious, automatic (behavioral-level) processing. Counterintuitively, slips happen *more* to experts than novices, precisely because expert performance is automated, so attention is more easily diverted at the junction where a wrong branch could be taken. Three sub-types matter most for interfaces:

- **Capture slips** — a more frequent or recent action sequence "captures" control at the point where it shares identical opening steps with the intended, rarer sequence (his colleague unbuckling his wristwatch instead of his seatbelt, out of habit). Fix: make rare-but-consequential procedures diverge from common ones as early as possible, not just where they differ semantically.
- **Description-similarity slips** — the correct action is performed on the wrong object because the mental description used to pick the target was ambiguous and a second object satisfies it equally well (a rolled-up shirt thrown in the toilet instead of the laundry basket because "large enough container" matched both). This connects to the "knowledge in the world" point that descriptions only need to be precise enough to distinguish the alternatives *currently present* — which is exactly why a fix that works today breaks the moment a new, similar-looking option joins a list or button set. Fix: make controls for meaningfully different actions look or read differently — never rely on position alone to distinguish a destructive row from a safe one.
- **Mode errors** — the same control means different things depending on a device state the user isn't tracking, unavoidable once a device has more meanings than controls. It worsens when an interruption separates mode-selection from use, or when two modes look similar but produce very different effects — Norman's Airbus example: entering "−3.3" meant either a −3.3° descent angle or −3,300 feet/minute depending on which of two similar autopilot modes was active, a fatal difference invisible in the number itself. Fix: eliminate modes where possible; where unavoidable, make the current mode continuously and saliently visible.
- **Memory-lapse slips** — an action, or its completion, is simply forgotten, usually via an interruption between deciding and finishing (leaving an original in a copier; leaving a bank card in an ATM after taking the cash). Fix: forcing functions again — withhold the object people rarely forget (the cash) until the object they might forget (the card) is reclaimed, since people almost never forget the actual goal, only the housekeeping steps.

A **mistake**, by contrast, happens when the goal or plan itself is wrong — flawless execution then just carries out a bad plan. Mistakes live in the top three stages (goal, plan, compare/evaluate), governed by conscious, reflective reasoning. Three sub-types:

- **Rule-based mistakes** — either the situation is misclassified and the wrong rule invoked, or the right rule is invoked but doesn't cover the actual situation (a rule written for routine operation with no override for an emergency — his nightclub-fire example, doors rigged against dine-and-dash exits with no fire-evacuation exception) — or the rule is followed correctly but its outcome is misread (a driver interpreting correctly-functioning anti-lock brake pulsing as malfunction and releasing the pedal).
- **Knowledge-based mistakes** — the situation is genuinely novel, no rule or skill applies, and reasoning from scratch is wrong because the underlying knowledge is erroneous or incomplete (the Gimli Glider accident: fuel weight computed in pounds where kilograms were required).
- **Memory-lapse mistakes** — not one forgotten step within an intact plan, but the goal, plan, or current evaluation of system state forgotten wholesale, usually via interruption, so a fresh and possibly wrong decision gets remade from nothing. These are harder to detect than memory-lapse slips: a slip still leaves an expectation to be violated by the missing step; a lost plan may leave no expectation to notice is missing.

**The load-bearing consequence, stated precisely because it is easy to flatten:** slips and mistakes require different, and often opposite, fixes, and using the wrong one is not merely unhelpful — it can be inert while looking like a fix. A confirmation dialog is genuinely effective against a slip, because the *surprise* of an unexpected prompt is itself the detection signal — Norman's own worked example is typing Command+W by accident instead of Shift+W: the confirmation dialog is unexpected, which is exactly what alerts him that a slip occurred, so he cancels. The identical dialog is nearly useless against a mistake, because the person's (wrong) plan already anticipated and endorses the action — in his second example, he deliberately closes a window he mistakenly believes is inactive, the confirmation matches his (wrong) expectation, and he clicks through it without reading, losing the work. Same UI element, same code path, opposite outcome, because the two error categories are being confused. This means "we added a confirmation dialog" is not an answer to "how do we prevent destructive mistakes" — it is only an answer to "how do we catch slips at the moment of divergence." Fixing mistakes requires acting upstream of the confirm click: keeping goal/plan/current-state information visible so the person's diagnosis of the situation is less likely to be wrong in the first place, and sensibility checks on the actual content of the action (see below) rather than friction on the act of confirming it.

## Designing for error

Norman's checklist for the code path itself: understand the causes of error and design to minimize them; run **sensibility checks** — does the requested action pass an automatic plausibility test given context (a hundred-fold currency transfer, a thousand-fold radiation dose) rather than executing exactly as typed; make actions **reversible** (Undo) wherever possible, and where an action truly cannot be reversed, make it deliberately harder to perform rather than compensating with confirmation alone; make errors easy to discover and correct; and treat unexpected input as an approximation to what the person wanted rather than a rejection-worthy error — help complete the real intent rather than blocking and restarting. Undo deserves separate billing from confirmation because it doesn't depend on the person noticing anything at decision time — it's the one fix here that helps against both slips and mistakes, since it acts after the fact rather than gating the moment of commitment.

On the systemic side, Norman (with James Reason) frames most real accidents through the **Swiss cheese model**: multiple independent layers of defense each have gaps, and a bad outcome requires the gaps in several layers to align simultaneously — there is essentially never a single root cause, only a chain where "if only" any one link had held, the outcome wouldn't have occurred. The corollary: root cause analysis shouldn't stop at the first human action found in the chain — that's typically where blame-seeking investigations stop, but it's where real analysis should begin, repeatedly asking "why" (Toyota's "Five Whys") past the first plausible answer. Norman concedes the technique isn't foolproof: "why" is ambiguous, investigators stop at the edge of their own understanding, and it still tempts a single-cause narrative even when the truth is a conjunction of factors.

This connects to a claim a developer might reasonably resist rather than accept flatly: Norman argues "human error" is usually a *design verdict*, not a fact about the operator — when 75–95% of industrial accidents get attributed to "human error," that ratio is itself evidence the system reliably produces this failure regardless of who's operating it, not evidence of incompetent people. But he does not claim people are never at fault: he explicitly concedes fatigue, intoxication, and knowing rule-violation are real, individually-attributable causes, which is why flight-hour and drinking regulations exist. The non-flattened version of his claim is narrower than "never blame the user": an investigation shouldn't be allowed to terminate at "user error" as if that explained anything — ask why the system made the error easy or undetectable first, and only then does "the person was also genuinely at fault" become a defensible residual conclusion.

Two further connections worth making explicit. **Checklists** reduce slips and memory lapses, but Norman's evidence is specifically for *collaborative, two-person* execution (one reads, one performs) — a single person executing then a second checking affords each less care, since each assumes the other will catch problems, so paradoxically more checkers can mean less attention per checker. A solo developer reviewing their own migration lacks this structure; the real substitute is an automated, tool-enforced check, not a mental note to be careful. The **paradox of automation** is effectively a mode error at systemic scale: automation handles routine cases and silently hands rare, hard cases back to a human who has been out of the loop and lost track of which mode the system is in — the cruise ship *Royal Majesty* ran aground because navigation had silently fallen back from GPS to dead-reckoning mode days earlier behind a tiny, easily-missed indicator, the same failure shape as a UI mode error, just at systemic scale.

## Knowledge in the head vs. in the world

Norman's tradeoff: precise behavior does not require precise memorized knowledge, because behavior draws on knowledge distributed across the head (learned conventions, procedural skill) and the world (physical constraints, visible labels, structure). Knowledge in the world is available without prior learning but costs search-and-interpretation time at the moment of use and clutters the interface; knowledge in the head is fast and effortless once learned but requires the learning investment and is unreliable unless something in the world triggers its recall ("out of sight, out of mind"). Crucially, descriptions and mental categories only ever need to be precise enough to distinguish among the alternatives *actually present* — which is why a system that has worked fine for years can suddenly produce description-similarity slips the moment a new, similar option is introduced (his coin-confusion examples: introducing a new coin the same size as an existing one broke discrimination that had worked for decades). The actionable version for interface design: put the knowledge required for basic operation into the world (visible labels, obvious state) so infrequent or new users don't need to have memorized anything, but don't force experts through that same friction once they've internalized it — support both paths rather than picking one. And where reminders are concerned, a reminder needs two separable components to work — the **signal** that something needs to be remembered, and the **message** of what it is — and most reminder mechanisms (a string around a finger; a note nobody looks at again) supply only one of the two.

## Conceptual models and system image

A **conceptual model** is a simplified explanation of how something works, and Norman is explicit that it does not need to be accurate — only useful for predicting the effect of an action (his colleague Sayeki's admittedly-wrong mental model for a motorcycle's turn signal, which nonetheless let him reliably operate it correctly). The **system image** is everything the user can actually perceive about a system — its visible structure, its behavior, its documentation, its error messages — and it is the *only* channel available for shaping the user's mental model, because the designer and the user never communicate directly; whatever the system image fails to convey, the user invents to fill the gap, and if the invented model is wrong, the user cannot succeed no matter how carefully they try. Norman's refrigerator example is the clean illustration: two labeled dials ("freezer," "refrigerator") strongly implied two independent thermostats, but the actual mechanism was one thermostat plus one air-diversion valve — a system image that actively suggested the wrong conceptual model, compounded by a 24-hour feedback delay that made empirically discovering the true model impractical. The design implication for a settings panel or any configuration surface: the labels, grouping, and visible structure of controls *are* the conceptual model as far as the user is concerned, and if that visible structure implies independence between two things that are actually coupled (or vice versa), no amount of accurate underlying implementation compensates — the system image itself has to state the correct causal model, not just avoid stating a false one.
